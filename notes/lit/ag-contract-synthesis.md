# Computing component contracts from a system-level contract: a survey of the assume–guarantee literature

Survey date: 2026-10-26. Every citation below was checked against a publisher page, DBLP-derived record, author page, or arXiv listing during this search. Where a field (pages, volume) could not be confirmed it is left out of the bibliography rather than guessed.

## 0. The question, restated in the notation of the survey

The setting is a feed-forward pair M₁ → M₂: a planner M₁ that does not yet exist and a waypoint controller M₂ whose C++ source is in hand. Contracts are unsaturated pairs (A, G) of behaviour sets, M ⊨ (A, G) iff M ∩ A ⊆ G, and the sequential rule combines A ⊆ A₁, G₁ ⊆ A₂, G₂ ∩ A₂ ⊆ G with the two local satisfaction facts to conclude M₁ ∩ M₂ ⊨ (A, G). The task is to compute (A₁, G₁) and (A₂, G₂) so that the second pair is one that M₂'s code can be shown to meet with an SMT-based bounded model checker. Two facts about this rule shape the reading of the literature. First, unsaturated and saturated contracts carry the same information about implementations, since M ⊨ (A, G) iff M ⊨ (A, G ∪ ¬A); most contract algebras work with the saturated representative, so their formulas transfer once one saturates. Second, the rule is the non-circular, one-directional special case of assume–guarantee composition: M₁'s obligation G₁ discharges M₂'s assumption A₂, and nothing flows back. That makes it close in spirit to the asymmetric rule used in the learning-based assume–guarantee literature (Section 2) and to the "missing component" problem treated by the quotient (Section 1).

A short derivation makes the target concrete and is used throughout as a yardstick. Fix the top-level (A, G) and the code M₂. If (A₂, G₂) is any contract that M₂ meets, then the tightest usable guarantee is G₂ = M₂ ∩ A₂ (up to saturation), and the third premise reduces to M₂ ∩ A₂ ⊆ G, i.e. A₂ ⊆ G ∪ ¬M₂. When A₂ is required to speak only about the interface between planner and controller (planner outputs, controller inputs, plus whatever environment signals both see), the largest admissible A₂ is the universal projection W₂ = {u : every behaviour of M₂ that agrees with u on the interface lies in G}. Every A₂ ⊆ W₂ is a correct delegate assumption for M₂, and given any such A₂ the most permissive demand on the planner is (A₁, G₁) = (A, A₂). Feasibility of the planner side then asks for some M₁ with M₁ ∩ A ⊆ A₂. So the feasible delegates form an interval: from above, A₂ is bounded by W₂, which is a function of M₂'s code and G alone; from below, A₂ must be large enough for some planner to land inside it under A. In the literature W₂ has a name, the weakest environment assumption, and the operation that produces the planner demand from (A, G) and M₂ is a quotient or residual. The rest of this survey reads each body of work against this picture.

## 1. Contract algebra: quotient, residual, and the missing component

### 1.1 Benveniste et al., "Contracts for System Design" (2018), and its antecedent (2008)

The monograph by Benveniste, Caillaud, Nickovic, Passerone, Raclet, Reinkemeier, Sangiovanni-Vincentelli, Damm, Henzinger and Larsen sets out a meta-theory in which a contract is characterised by its set of admissible environments and its set of admissible implementations, and refinement, composition, conjunction and quotient are defined by order-theoretic optimality (composition as the least contract met by any composition of implementations, quotient as the greatest contract whose composition with a given one refines a target). It then instantiates the meta-theory as assume–guarantee contracts over behaviour sets, as interface automata, and as modal interfaces, and connects them. For assume–guarantee contracts over sets the operations are Boolean expressions in A, G and their complements, so their cost is exactly the cost of Boolean operations (including complement) in whatever representation one uses for behaviour sets; for automata-based instances complement brings determinisation. Implementations enter only as the objects that satisfy contracts: the theory supplies the satisfaction relation but no procedure that reads an implementation to choose a contract, and the choice of a component contract is left to designers, who then check it. The earlier FMCO paper by Benveniste, Caillaud, Ferrari, Mangeruca, Passerone and Sofronis introduced the assume–guarantee model of this school and the idea of several viewpoints (function, timing, safety) carried as separate contracts on one component. For the present question these works fix vocabulary and supply closed forms; they do not themselves narrow the space of delegates by looking at code.

### 1.2 Raclet's residual (2008) and modal interface quotients (2011)

Raclet's FACS 2007 paper, published in ENTCS in 2008, defines a residual S/S₁ for modal and acceptance specifications: the residual is the specification whose implementations are exactly the components C₂ such that, for every implementation C₁ of S₁, the product C₁ × C₂ meets S. Inputs are two specifications (the global one and the one for the known part); the output is a specification for the unknown part. Raclet, Badouel, Benveniste, Caillaud, Legay and Passerone then built a modal interface theory that combines the optimistic compatibility of interface automata with the richer algebra of modal specifications, with quotient among its operations. In both papers the known part enters as a specification; it may be taken to be the most precise modal specification of a concrete automaton, in which case the residual is computed from the implementation itself. Computation is automata-theoretic: quotient involves determinising the specification being divided, so the worst case is exponential in that specification, and everything is finite-state.

### 1.3 Incer, Sangiovanni-Vincentelli, Lin, Kang, "Quotient for Assume-Guarantee Contracts" (MEMOCODE 2018)

This paper supplies the closed form of the quotient for assume–guarantee contracts. Given a top-level contract C = (A, G) and the contract C′ = (A′, G′) of the part already chosen, the quotient C/C′ is the largest contract C″ with C′ ‖ C″ ⪯ C; in saturated form it has assumption A ∩ G′ and guarantee (A′ ∩ G) ∪ ¬(A ∩ G′), so any contract that refines it is an acceptable spec for the missing component. The inputs are two contracts, not an implementation. If one takes C′ to be the tightest contract of the existing code, (everything, M₂), the quotient becomes (A ∩ M₂, G ∪ ¬(A ∩ M₂)): the missing component must ensure G whenever it runs beside M₂ under A. That is correct but not in the shape the sequential rule wants, because it still mentions M₂'s internal behaviour; turning it into a demand on interface signals alone is precisely the projection step that produces W₂ in Section 0. The paper assumes saturated contracts over a Boolean algebra of behaviours, and the computational cost is that of complement and intersection in the chosen representation.

### 1.4 Íncer Romeo, Mangeruca, Villa, Sangiovanni-Vincentelli, "The Quotient in Preorder Theories" (GandALF 2020)

The authors abstract the quotient to any preorder with a monotone multiplication and an involution, give a condition (admissibility) under which the largest solution of A·x ≤ B exists, and show that it then has a uniform closed form. Their "preordered heaps" include Boolean lattices, assume–guarantee contracts and interface automata, so known quotients are recovered, and "sieved heaps" handle structures defined over several domains. Inputs are algebraic elements; implementations enter only insofar as an implementation is itself an element of the structure (for example a behaviour set), in which case one can divide by it directly. The contribution is existence and form, not an algorithm for any particular symbolic representation.

### 1.5 Passerone, Incer, Sangiovanni-Vincentelli, contract merging and separation (ACM TECS 2019)

This EMSOFT 2019 paper (ACM TECS) revisits weak versus strong assumptions and maximal specifications, decomposes conjunction and disjunction into simpler steps, and from these derives a merging operator meant to reflect design intent better than plain conjunction, along with separation, the adjoint of merging, used to split a specification across viewpoints. It points out symmetries between composition/quotient and merging/separation. The operations are again closed forms over saturated contracts on sets. For the present problem separation is the viewpoint counterpart of the quotient: it would let one peel, for instance, a timing viewpoint off the top-level contract before delegating it. No implementation enters.

### 1.6 Hypercontracts (NFM 2022; FMSD 2025) and the algebraic follow-ups

Incer, Benveniste, Sangiovanni-Vincentelli and Seshia define hypercontracts, where the component model is richer than a set of traces (a set of sets, enough for hyperproperties such as information flow), and show that refinement, composition and quotient keep closed forms in this setting. Conic hypercontracts give a generic but finitely describable subclass that specialises to assume–guarantee contracts and to interface automata. Incer's dissertation, "The Algebra of Contracts", gathers these results and adds operations beyond composition, conjunction and disjunction; a 2024 note by Incer shows that composition and merging are both tensor products of the contract algebra. These works widen what a "behaviour set" can be but leave the choice of a component contract to the designer; their relevance here is that the quotient survives richer semantics, so if the top-level query were a hyperproperty (for instance noninterference between planner inputs and controller outputs) the same decomposition pattern would apply.

### 1.7 Pacti (arXiv 2023; ACM TCPS 2025)

Pacti, by Incer, Badithela, Graebener, Mallozzi, Pandey, Yu, Benveniste, Caillaud, Murray, Sangiovanni-Vincentelli and Seshia, is the most concrete computational treatment of the quotient. Contracts are input–output contracts whose assumptions and guarantees are conjunctions of linear inequalities (polyhedra) over named input and output variables. The paper's central observation is that the exact closed forms blow up formula size, and since the quotient is a greatest element, any refinement of it is also a valid spec for the missing component; Pacti therefore returns a simplified refinement (or, for composition, a relaxation), eliminating variables by computing refinements and relaxations of linear inequalities and removing redundant terms, with containment checked by linear programming. The case studies include one directly analogous to the present question: in an autonomy stack with an object detector feeding a controller, the controller's contract is first obtained (lower bounds fitted by a linear program from a model of the controller), and the quotient of the system-level safety contract by that controller contract yields a requirement on the detector. So in Pacti the implementation of the known component enters through a contract that has been extracted from it beforehand, not by dividing by the code. Pacti's authors list contract satisfaction checking of a component as not yet supported. The polyhedral restriction is close to the linear predicates used for the delegate problem, but there is no temporal structure in the operations themselves.

### 1.8 Earlier "missing component" formulations: Merlin–Bochmann (1983), Bochmann (2002), Villa et al. (2012)

The oldest version of the question is the submodule construction problem. Merlin and Bochmann (TOPLAS 1983) ask for the specification of one missing module given the global specification and the other modules, all described by sets of execution sequences, give a general formula for the largest solution, and a constructive algorithm when everything is finite-state. Bochmann's FORTE 2002 paper recasts the problem with relational-database operations, shows that several known finite-automaton algorithms are instances, and gives a solution formula for the case where specifications carry input assumptions and output guarantees with inputs separated from outputs, plus an algorithm for synchronous, partially defined input/output automata. The book by Villa, Yevtushenko, Brayton, Mishchenko, Petrenko and Sangiovanni-Vincentelli, "The Unknown Component Problem", treats the same equation for finite-state machines under synchronous and parallel composition as language equations, with a software package, and relates it to supervisory control and games. In all three the known component enters as its own model, not as a contract: the largest solution is computed from the model of the known part and the global specification, which is the closest the classical contract-theory line comes to "the code bounds the feasible delegates". Complexity: the language-equation solution needs complementation of the composed behaviour, so it is exponential in the worst case (subset construction), and the setting is finite-state; nothing here handles real-valued signals or C++ directly.

### 1.9 Refinement checking and decomposition tools: OCRA (2015) and Iannopollo et al. (DATE 2018)

Cimatti and Tonetta give a proof system for contract refinement over component hierarchies in which contracts are temporal-logic formulas; correctness of a decomposition is reduced to a set of temporal entailment checks discharged with SMT-based model checking (the OCRA tool on top of nuXmv/NuSMV). Inputs are the hierarchy and the contracts of every component; the tool checks a proposed decomposition and does not compute one, and implementations enter only in a separate step where a component's model is checked against its contract. Iannopollo, Tripakis and Sangiovanni-Vincentelli do compute a decomposition: given an LTL assume–guarantee contract, they split it into smaller sub-specifications that can be synthesized independently from a library of components, discovering the split by analysing counterexamples that a model checker returns on a carefully constructed formula. The library is a set of contracts, so code enters only through the library contracts.

## 2. Assumption generation and learning for assume–guarantee reasoning

### 2.1 Giannakopoulou, Păsăreanu, Barringer: the weakest assumption (ASE 2002; journal version 2005)

This is the reference point for "compute the delegate assumption from the component itself". Given a component M (a labelled transition system), a safety property P, and the interface alphabet M shares with its environment, the procedure returns one of three outcomes: M meets P in every environment, M violates P in every environment, or an assumption automaton that admits exactly those environments in which M meets P. The assumption is the weakest one over the interface alphabet in the precise sense that, for every environment E, E composed with M meets P iff E meets the assumption. The construction composes M with the error automaton of P, propagates the error state backwards across internal actions, hides non-interface actions, determinises, and prunes; the determinisation makes it exponential in the worst case. The implementation is the input here: the assumption is a function of M's model and P. In Section 0's terms this is exactly W₂ computed from M₂, restricted to finite-state models and safety properties over shared actions.

### 2.2 L*-based assumption learning: Cobleigh, Giannakopoulou, Păsăreanu (TACAS 2003) and Păsăreanu et al. (FMSD 2008)

Instead of computing the weakest assumption outright, Cobleigh, Giannakopoulou and Păsăreanu learn an assumption with Angluin's L* for the asymmetric rule ⟨A⟩M₁⟨P⟩ and ⟨true⟩M₂⟨A⟩. Membership queries ask whether a trace over the interface keeps M₁ safe (answered by model checking M₁ on that trace); a conjectured A is checked by two model-checking runs, one per premise, and a counterexample either refines A or is shown to be a real violation of the composed system. Because the weakest assumption is regular, the learner terminates with at most that assumption, and frequently stops earlier with a smaller one that already suffices. The FMSD 2008 journal paper by Păsăreanu, Giannakopoulou, Gheorghiu Bobaru, Cobleigh and Barringer extends this to circular and n-way rules and adds alphabet refinement, which learns which interface actions the assumption must mention. Both components' models enter: one answers queries for the assumption, the other checks that it guarantees the assumption. Complexity follows L*: polynomially many queries in the size of the minimal assumption automaton and counterexample length, but each query is a model-checking run and the target can be exponential in the component. This is the closest classical match to the delegate setting: the learned A is a delegate assumption for one component and a delegate guarantee for the other, and it is driven by the implementations, not chosen freely.

### 2.3 Symbolic and variant learners: Alur–Madhusudan–Nam (CAV 2005), Chaki et al. (CAV 2005), Chaki–Strichman (TACAS 2007), Gupta–McMillan–Fu (CAV 2007), Gheorghiu Bobaru et al. (CAV 2008)

Alur, Madhusudan and Nam implement assumption learning symbolically inside NuSMV, so queries are BDD-based model-checking runs on hardware-style models, and report savings over monolithic symbolic checking. Chaki, Clarke, Sinha and Thati move from trace containment to simulation conformance, show that a weakest assumption still exists and is canonically a deterministic tree automaton, and learn it in time polynomial in the minimal such automaton with a model checker as teacher. Chaki and Strichman reduce the number and cost of queries in the L*-based loop. Gupta, McMillan and Fu compute an exact minimal assumption automaton that separates what one component must exclude from what the other may do, using sampling and a SAT solver; finding the smallest separating automaton is a hard combinatorial problem, and the payoff is small assumptions on hardware-like benchmarks. Gheorghiu Bobaru, Păsăreanu and Giannakopoulou replace learning with abstraction refinement: the assumption is a conservative, possibly nondeterministic abstraction of one component, refined using counterexamples from checking it with the other. In the last of these the assumption is literally an abstraction of an implementation, which is a direct form of "the code bounds the delegate": the delegate for M₁ is an over-approximation of what M₂ can do, refined until it is precise enough.

### 2.4 From design-level assumptions to source code: Giannakopoulou, Păsăreanu, Cobleigh (ICSE 2004)

This paper generates assumptions on design models, then uses them to check the source code of each component (Java via Java PathFinder) against its part, so that assumptions learned on cheap models are reused as properties or environments when the code is analysed. Code enters in the satisfaction check, and the design model enters in the generation; the mismatch between the two levels is handled by checking the code against the assumption-derived property. It is a direct precedent for using a code-level checker to accept or reject a delegate chosen at a higher level.

### 2.5 Game-theoretic assumption computation: Chatterjee–Henzinger (TACAS 2007), Anand et al. (TACAS 2023), Finkbeiner–Geier–Passing (NFM 2021)

When the components are to be synthesized rather than verified, assumption computation becomes a game question. Chatterjee and Henzinger define assume–guarantee synthesis for two processes with their own objectives, requiring each to meet its goal while the pair meets the conjunction, solved via secure equilibria on graph games; Peterson's protocol falls out of mutual-exclusion requirements. Anand, Mallik, Nayak and Schmuck compute, for an ω-regular objective of the system player on a finite game graph, an environment assumption that is sufficient (any compliant environment lets the system win), implementable (the environment can always comply), and permissive (it rules out no cooperative choice), with a polynomial-time symbolic algorithm for parity games. That trio of conditions is the game version of choosing A₂: sufficiency is the upper bound W₂, implementability is the planner-side lower bound, and permissiveness asks for the largest choice. Finkbeiner, Geier and Passing split an LTL specification into independent sub-specifications by analysing dependencies between variables and formula parts, so that separate synthesis runs compose correctly; this is decomposition without explicit assumptions, applicable when the parts do not need to constrain each other. In all three the component is given as a game arena or a specification, never as C++ code, and the decidability story rests on finite-state arenas.

## 3. Interface automata and interface synthesis from code

### 3.1 de Alfaro and Henzinger, interface automata and interface theories (FSE 2001; EMSOFT 2001)

Interface automata capture, in one automaton, what a component assumes about the order in which its inputs arrive and what it guarantees about its outputs. Two interfaces are compatible if some environment can use them together without reaching an error, composition is optimistic (it keeps exactly the states from which the environment can avoid errors), and refinement is alternating simulation; composition and refinement checks are polynomial in the product. The companion EMSOFT paper separates component models from interface models and argues that interface models, unlike component models, support compositional refinement and hence component-based design. Implementations do not enter: interfaces are written, then checked for compatibility. The quotient for interface automata was later obtained in the frameworks of Sections 1.2 and 1.4.

### 3.2 Interface synthesis from code: Alur–Černý–Madhusudan–Nam (POPL 2005) and Henzinger–Jhala–Majumdar (FSE 2005)

These two papers read code to produce the interface. Alur, Černý, Madhusudan and Nam extract, for a Java class and a safety requirement on its internal state, the set of method-call sequences a client may issue: they build a finite abstraction by predicate abstraction, pose interface construction as a partial-information two-player game on that abstraction, and approximate its solution with L* and symbolic model checking (the JIST tool). Henzinger, Jhala and Majumdar define an interface as safe if it excludes every call sequence that breaks the library's invariants and permissive if it admits every sequence that does not, and synthesise interfaces that are both, as graphs whose nodes carry predicates over the library state and whose edges are calls, refined by counterexamples inside the BLAST software model checker. In both, the implementation is the primary input, and the output is the most permissive (or a safe and permissive) assumption on clients, which is the client-side analogue of W₂. Since code is infinite-state, these are semi-algorithms whose termination depends on finding enough predicates; soundness comes from the model checker.

### 3.3 Symbolic interface learning: Giannakopoulou–Rakamarić–Raman (SAS 2012) and Howar–Giannakopoulou–Rakamarić (ISSTA 2013)

Psyco learns, for a white-box component with marked unsafe states, an interface whose transitions are method calls guarded by constraints on their parameters; L* proposes the automaton skeleton and symbolic execution answers queries and generates the guards automatically through refinement. Interfaces are three-valued (safe, unsafe, unresolved by the symbolic engine), which is an honest record of what a bounded analysis could and could not settle. The ISSTA 2013 follow-up mixes static, dynamic and symbolic analysis to answer queries more cheaply. Code enters at every query, and the guard constraints are SMT formulas over parameters, which is the nearest analogue in this literature to bounding a delegate assumption by SMT queries on the known component's code. Correctness of the final interface relied on testing in the released tool, as its later maintainers note.

### 3.4 Specifications for unknown procedures: Albarghouthi–Dillig–Gurfinkel (POPL 2016) and Prabhu et al. (PLDI 2021)

These are not usually filed under contracts, but they solve the delegate equation with the roles exactly as in the present question: the code that exists is given, the code that does not exist is a hole, and the task is the most permissive specification of the hole under which the whole program meets its postcondition. Albarghouthi, Dillig and Gurfinkel call this maximal specification inference, run a counterexample-guided loop, and reduce each step to multi-abduction, a generalisation of logical abduction, solved with quantifier elimination over linear arithmetic. Prabhu, Fedyukovich, Madhukar and D'Souza (HornSpec) work over constrained Horn clauses, add non-vacuity (the safety assertions must stay reachable, so the specification cannot be made trivially safe by being empty), and discover inductive invariants for loops along the way. In both, the existing code is the input that bounds the space of acceptable specifications for the missing part, maximality matches "largest A₂", and non-vacuity matches the requirement that a planner must still be able to meet the demand. Their scope is sequential first-order programs with unbounded verification via Horn solving, not bounded unrolling of a reactive loop, and the decidability story is that of the underlying theory and Horn solving (undecidable in general, effective in practice on linear arithmetic).

## 4. Contracts for cyber-physical systems with STL or signal specifications

### 4.1 Nuzzo, Sangiovanni-Vincentelli and colleagues: methodology (IEEE Access 2014; Proc. IEEE 2015) and stochastic contracts (arXiv 2017)

The aircraft electric power system paper by Nuzzo, Xu, Ozay, Finn, Sangiovanni-Vincentelli, Murray, Donzé and Seshia separates topology from control protocol, writes requirements in LTL, STL and arithmetic constraints, and places assume–guarantee contracts at the hand-off points between design steps; the platform-based methodology paper by Nuzzo, Sangiovanni-Vincentelli, Bresolin, Geretti and Villa generalises this to a contract-driven design flow for cyber-physical systems with tool support at each layer. Contracts are chosen by designers from the requirements and then verified with model checkers, SMT/MILP-based checks and simulation-based falsification; the implementation enters at verification, and some flows synthesize the controller against its contract. Li, Nuzzo, Sangiovanni-Vincentelli, Xi and Li extend this to stochastic contracts written in a stochastic variant of STL and verified by convex or mixed-integer programs. None of these computes a delegate from code; they show what checks are tractable once a delegate is proposed.

### 4.2 Parametric and convex contracts from dynamics: Kim–Arcak–Seshia (HSCC 2017) and Ghasemi–Sadraddini–Belta (HSCC 2020; arXiv 2022)

Kim, Arcak and Seshia make the guarantee depend on a parameter of the assumption, so a component promises a bound that is a function of how well-behaved its environment is, and prove a small-gain theorem that closes the loop on interconnected parametric contracts; they apply it to a parametrised fragment of temporal logic on a traffic network. Ghasemi, Sadraddini and Belta parameterise polytopic contracts on states and inputs of linear subsystems so that the set of parameters that compose correctly is convex, and break joint synthesis into small convex programs per subsystem plus a potential function that measures distance from correct composition; their 2022 follow-up targets STL specifications under perturbations. In both, the component's dynamics (its implementation, in the sense of a plant-and-controller model) enters as constraints that the contract parameters must satisfy, so the feasible contracts are carved out by the model. This is the CPS analogue of the code bounding the delegate, with a linear model rather than C++ code, and with tractability coming from convexity rather than model checking.

### 4.3 Continuous-time and STL contract decomposition: Saoud–Girard–Fribourg (Automatica 2021), Liu et al. (CDC 2022; arXiv 2023), Kanno–Hoshino–Hatanaka (arXiv 2026)

Saoud, Girard and Fribourg give assume–guarantee contracts for continuous-time systems with conditions under which circular composition of invariance-type contracts is sound. Liu, Saoud, Jagtap, Dimarogonas and Zamani express a fragment of STL through funnel-based control as assume–guarantee contracts on interconnected nonlinear systems, define a satisfaction notion under which local contracts entail the global one, and design closed-form decentralised controllers; the 2023 follow-up by Liu, Saoud and Dimarogonas handles collaborative multi-agent STL tasks. Kanno, Hoshino and Hatanaka derive a necessary and sufficient condition for local STL contracts to decompose a global specification under a given partition of agents, and a condition under which the decomposition is valid for every partition, with tube-based synthesis for linear dynamics and affine predicates. In this line the local contracts are fixed by the decomposition scheme (funnels, partitions), the controller is then synthesized to meet them, and implementations do not constrain the contract choice except through the class of dynamics assumed.

### 4.4 Mission-level STL decomposed into contracts: Waqas, Naik, Ioannou, Nuzzo (arXiv 2022)

A mission specification in an STL fragment is cut into predicates on non-overlapping time intervals, each mapped to a contract built from a time-varying control barrier function, and the barrier functions constrain a quadratic-programming controller. This is the nearest published example of decomposing a mission-level STL requirement into contracts for a planner-to-controller hierarchy. The decomposition is by the time structure of the formula; the controller is synthesized to meet each contract, so the controller's implementation follows the contract rather than bounding it.

### 4.5 Interface-aware STL: Ferrère, Nickovic, Donzé, Ito, Kapinski (HSCC 2019)

The paper adds an explicit input/output signature to STL formulas, so that a formula can be read as a contract (constraints on inputs as assumptions, on outputs as guarantees), and defines robustness and vacuity notions that respect this split; it is implemented in Breach and applied to automotive models. For the delegate problem it supplies the syntactic separation that makes "A₂ talks only about controller inputs" well-defined for STL.

### 4.6 Mining assumptions from the model: Mohammadinejad–Deshmukh–Puranic (ICCPS 2020) and Gaaloul et al. (ESEC/FSE 2020; TSE 2022)

Mohammadinejad, Deshmukh and Puranic learn an STL environment assumption for a component whose output requirement is given in STL: simulated input signals are labelled good or bad according to whether the corresponding outputs meet the requirement, and the assumption is the classifier, found by enumerating parametric STL shapes and fitting parameters with decision trees. The implementation enters as the simulator that labels data; nothing certifies the result. Gaaloul, Menghi, Nejati, Briand and Wolfe (EPIcuRus) close this gap for Simulink models: search-based test generation explores inputs, a learner (decision trees, later genetic programming and random search in the TSE paper with Isasi Parache) proposes an assumption over input signals, and an industrial exhaustive model checker for Simulink (QVtrace) is used both for a sanity check (is the requirement neither always met nor always violated?) and to prove that the proposed assumption is sound, with test feedback steering the next round. The TSE paper makes explicit a trade-off between soundness and informativeness: weaker, more informative assumptions are harder for the checker to prove, and the checker can return inconclusive verdicts. This is the closest working system to the present question in the CPS literature: a component's implementation is checked by a solver-backed model checker, the assumption on its inputs is proposed by a search procedure, and only assumptions the checker certifies are kept. What it lacks for the delegate setting is the other side, namely a top-level contract to be split and a planner whose feasibility bounds the assumption from below.

### 4.7 Computing the contract of a model: the Refinement Calculus of Reactive Systems toolset (Dragomir, Preoteasa, Tripakis)

RCRS represents components as monotonic property transformers, a refinement-calculus form of contract that records both the inputs a component accepts and the input–output relation it ensures. Its toolset translates Simulink diagrams into RCRS terms, composes them in Isabelle, simplifies the result, and checks properties such as compatibility (whether some input is accepted by the composite); a fuel-control model was shown compatible this way. The implementation is the input: the contract of a block diagram is computed from the diagram, including the largest input set on which it behaves, so the assumption side of M₂'s contract is derived rather than guessed. The cost is symbolic simplification in a theorem prover, which may need human help for nonlinear blocks.

### 4.8 Robotic mission decomposition with contracts: CROME (MEMOCODE 2020), Mallozzi et al. (FormaliSE 2023; arXiv 2023)

CROME, by Mallozzi, Pelliccione, Nuzzo and Schneider, organises robot mission goals as a contract-based goal graph with LTL contracts, checks mission feasibility, refines goals from a library, synthesizes controllers, and, when the whole mission is unrealizable, identifies realizable sub-missions per context. Mallozzi, Incer, Nuzzo and Sangiovanni-Vincentelli use quotient, separation, composition and merging to model, refine and repair mission specifications against a library of pre-implemented specifications: when the library cannot meet the specification they compute an approximation achievable from the library and either search for the missing part (via the quotient) or repair the requirement. Mallozzi, Nuzzo, Piterman, Schneider and Pelliccione decompose contextual missions into context-specific sub-missions with contracts and handle switching between their controllers. Here pre-implemented components enter as library contracts, and the quotient is used exactly as in the delegate question, with the library playing the role of M₂; the library contracts are given, not derived from code.

## 5. Where M₂'s implementation enters the choice of (A₂, G₂)

Read against Section 0, the literature splits into three groups. In the contract-algebra line (Benveniste et al., Raclet, Incer et al., Passerone et al., hypercontracts, Pacti, OCRA, CROME and its successors), the known component appears as a contract, and the implementation enters only at satisfaction checking, after a designer or a fitting step has chosen the contract; the quotient then turns the top-level contract and the known contract into the weakest demand on the missing part. Pacti's autonomy case study is the clearest instance of this two-step pattern: a contract is fitted to the controller first, then the system contract is divided by it. In the assumption-generation line (Giannakopoulou et al. 2002, Cobleigh et al., Păsăreanu et al., Alur et al., Chaki et al., Gupta et al., Gheorghiu Bobaru et al.), the implementation is the input: the weakest assumption, or a learned assumption between the strongest needed and the weakest admissible, is computed from the known component's model and the property, and the learner's conjectures are accepted or refuted by model checking that model. In the code-level interface and specification synthesis line (Alur–Černý–Madhusudan–Nam, Henzinger–Jhala–Majumdar, Psyco, maximal specification synthesis, HornSpec) the input is actual source code and the output is the most permissive constraint on whatever interacts with it, computed by abstraction refinement, symbolic execution with SMT, or abduction. The CPS line mostly fixes contracts by a decomposition scheme and synthesizes controllers to them, with two exceptions where the model shapes the contract: parametric or convex contracts whose feasible parameters are cut out by the dynamics (Kim et al., Ghasemi et al.), and assumption mining for Simulink with a solver-backed model checker certifying each candidate (EPIcuRus); RCRS computes the contract of a model symbolically.

The closest existing thing to "the code bounds the feasible delegates" is therefore the weakest-assumption construction of Giannakopoulou, Păsăreanu and Barringer and its learning-based variants: they compute, from the known component's implementation and the property, the largest admissible assumption over the shared interface, and every correct delegate assumption lies below it. The nearest code-level counterparts are permissive interface synthesis (BLAST), Psyco's SMT-guarded interfaces, and maximal specification synthesis for unknown procedures, which compute the same kind of upper bound from source code; the nearest CPS counterpart with a solver-backed checker in the loop is EPIcuRus. None of these puts the two halves together for a temporal, real-valued top-level contract: they bound the assumption of the known part from above but do not combine that bound with a quotient of a top-level STL-style contract and a feasibility requirement on the yet-unwritten planner, and none uses bounded model checking of C++ as the oracle that accepts or refutes candidate delegates. In the literature found here, the interval picture (W₂ from the code as an upper bound, planner realizability as a lower bound, quotient to pass the bound to the planner's guarantee) is implicit in the pieces but not stated or computed as one procedure.
