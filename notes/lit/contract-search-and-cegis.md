# Contract search, CEGIS, and learned proposers: a survey for two-component delegation

Scope: the synthesis and search literature relevant to computing a pair of assume–guarantee contracts for a series composition M₁ → M₂, where M₂ is existing C++ (ArduPilot waypoint navigation) checked by an SMT-based bounded model checker and M₁ is a planner still to be built. Citations were checked against arXiv, Crossref, DBLP-derived listings and publisher or author pages (October 2026). Each entry says what the method searches over, what checks a candidate, what the loop guarantees, and how far it scales.

## 0. The problem, restated so the literature can be mapped onto it

Write the delegation conditions as A ⊆ A₁, G₁ ⊆ A₂, G₂ ∩ A₂ ⊆ G, M₂ ∩ A₂ ⊆ G₂, M₁ ∩ A₁ ⊆ G₁. Two simplifications follow directly from these inclusions and shape everything below. First, once (A₂, G₂) is fixed, the most lenient obligation that can be handed to M₁ is (A₁, G₁) = (A, A₂): any admissible (A₁, G₁) has A₁ ⊇ A and G₁ ⊆ A₂, so M₁ ∩ A₁ ⊆ G₁ forces M₁ ∩ A ⊆ A₂. Step two is therefore not a search; it is the contract quotient (Section 4) and has a closed form in this rule. Second, once A₂ is fixed, the weakest admissible guarantee for M₂ is G ∪ ¬A₂, so the program obligation reduces to M₂ ∩ A₂ ⊆ G. The whole problem thus collapses onto a single unknown, the interface A₂ (a PolySTL formula over the planner-to-controller signals), subject to two conditions: (C) M₂ ∩ A₂ ⊆ G, which is a program-verification query and is downward closed in A₂ (shrinking A₂ never breaks it), and (R) there exists an implementable M₁ with M₁ ∩ A ⊆ A₂, which is a realizability query and is upward closed in A₂ (enlarging A₂ never breaks it). Delegation succeeds exactly when some A₂ in the grammar lies between what the planner needs and what the controller tolerates. A side condition is that A₂ should constrain only M₂'s inputs; an A₂ that restricts M₂'s outputs makes (C) vacuous, which is the usual receptiveness requirement in contract theory.

The quantifier structure is then: ∃A₂ [∀ behaviours of M₂ with inputs in A₂: G holds] ∧ [∃M₁ ∀ environment behaviours in A: M₁'s output is in A₂]. The first bracket is the exists–forall the question names. The second is itself a forall–exists (a game) unless M₁ is fixed, in which case it becomes another forall check. The sections below are organised around who proposes A₂, who refutes it, and how refutations flow back.

## 1. Counterexample-guided inductive synthesis and syntax-guided synthesis

**Solar-Lezama et al., SKETCH (ASPLOS 2006).** The search space is the set of completions of a partial program: the user writes a program with integer-valued holes and the tool fills them. Candidates are produced by a SAT solver over a finite set of inputs; a second SAT query checks the completed program against the specification on all inputs in a bounded domain and returns a failing input if there is one, which is added to the finite set. This is the original CEGIS loop: an inductive learner that only needs to satisfy finitely many examples, alternating with a deductive verifier that supplies new examples. Guarantees: sound with respect to the bounded verifier; complete and terminating because the hole domain and input domain are finite bit-vectors. Scalability is governed by how many counterexamples are needed, which in practice is often small compared with the input space; the AES case study filled the hard part of a cipher implementation in about an hour.

**Alur et al., Syntax-guided synthesis (FMCAD 2013).** SyGuS fixes a standard problem format: a background theory, a logical specification, and a context-free grammar of admissible terms; the goal is a term from the grammar that satisfies the specification in the theory. The search is over grammar derivations. The paper describes three CEGIS instantiations (enumerative, symbolic constraint-based, stochastic), all checked by an SMT solver that either validates the candidate or returns a counterexample input. Guarantees: sound by the SMT check; completeness is relative to the grammar and only semi-decision in general (enumeration terminates if a solution exists in the grammar and the theory is decidable). Scalability depends on the grammar's size and the depth of the needed term; enumerative solvers do well on small terms and poorly on terms with non-trivial constants. For contract search this is the cleanest formal framing: the PolySTL fragment allowed for A₂ is a SyGuS grammar, and (C) and (R) are the specification.

**Abate, David, Kesseli, Kroening, Polgreen, CEGIS(T) (CAV 2018).** The weakness of plain CEGIS on real-valued constants is that each counterexample only rules out one constant. CEGIS(T) splits a candidate into a syntactic skeleton and symbolic constants; when the verifier refutes a candidate, a theory solver generalises the refutation into a constraint on the constants (via Fourier–Motzkin elimination or a first-order satisfiability call) so that a whole region of constants is pruned. Checker: an SMT or bounded-model-checking backend over the full domain. Guarantees: sound by the verifier; termination improves for linear arithmetic because the constant space is eliminated symbolically rather than sampled. The method was adopted inside CVC4's SyGuS engine. Relevance: A₂ will have a small skeleton and real constants (distances, speeds, turn angles), which is exactly the regime CEGIS(T) targets.

**Kong, Solar-Lezama, Gao, δ-decision procedures for exists–forall over the reals (CAV 2018).** This gives a δ-complete procedure for ∃x ∀y φ(x, y) with nonlinear real arithmetic (including transcendental functions) by nesting a CEGIS-style loop inside the branch-and-prune engine of dReal, using local numerical optimisation to find good counterexamples quickly. Guarantees: the answer is either a certified "unsat" or "δ-sat", meaning a δ-weakening of the formula holds; the procedure terminates for bounded domains. Scalability is modest in the number of universally quantified variables. Relevance: once the program check is replaced by its finite trace consequences (see Section 8), the pure-logic part of the delegation problem is a bounded exists–forall over polynomial real arithmetic, which is this tool's input language.

**Ravanbakhsh and Sankaranarayanan, CEGIS for control Lyapunov functions (CDC 2015).** The search is over the coefficients of a fixed-shape polynomial certificate for a switched system; the learner solves an LMI relaxation over finitely many sampled states and the verifier (nonlinear SMT, or an LMI-based check) looks for a state where the certificate conditions fail. Guarantees: a returned certificate is valid; termination is argued by shrinking the candidate coefficient region with each counterexample (a volume argument). This is the control-community version of CEGIS over reals and shows that the learner can be a convex program rather than a SAT/SMT call.

**Chang, Roohi, Gao, Neural Lyapunov control (NeurIPS 2019).** The proposer is a neural network trained by gradient descent on a loss that penalises Lyapunov-condition violations at sampled states; the checker is a δ-complete SMT falsifier that searches for violating states, which are added to the training set. Guarantees: if the falsifier finds no violation, the conditions hold on the verified domain (up to δ); no completeness or termination guarantee for the learner. Relevance: an early clear instance of a learned, unsound proposer paired with a sound checker, the same division of labour the LLM work in Section 6 uses.

## 2. Parameter synthesis and requirement mining for temporal logic

**Asarin, Donzé, Maler, Nickovic, Parametric identification of temporal properties (RV 2011, LNCS 7186, 2012).** Introduces parametric STL (PSTL), where thresholds and time bounds are variables. Given a signal and a PSTL formula, the method computes the validity domain: the set of parameter values for which the formula holds on that signal. One implementation is exact, using quantifier elimination in linear arithmetic; the other approximates the domain by adaptive search over parameter space. Guarantees: exact (first method) or approximate with refinement (second) for each signal. Scalability: exact elimination grows quickly with nesting; sampling grows with parameter dimension. The key structural fact later work relies on is monotonicity: for many PSTL shapes, satisfaction is monotone in each parameter, so the feasible region is an upward- or downward-closed set and its boundary can be found by bisection.

**Jin, Donzé, Deshmukh, Seshia, Mining requirements from closed-loop control models (HSCC 2013; IEEE TCAD 34(11), 2015).** Searches over parameter valuations of a user-chosen PSTL shape for the tightest values consistent with a closed-loop Simulink model. The loop alternates two steps: (i) given a finite set of simulation traces, find the tightest parameter valuation satisfied by all of them (an optimisation that exploits monotonicity); (ii) a falsifier (simulation-based robustness minimisation, Breach) tries to find an input producing a trace that violates the candidate; violating traces are added and step (i) repeats. Guarantees: the result is consistent with all traces seen and survived falsification, but falsification is not exhaustive, so the mined requirement is a high-confidence candidate rather than a proof. Scalability: the expensive part is simulation; the method was demonstrated on automotive powertrain models. Relevance: this is structurally the closest existing loop to computing G₂ for a fixed A₂ (mine the strongest guarantee of M₂), and swapping the falsifier for a bounded model checker on code turns the mined candidate into a checked one.

**Bartocci, Deshmukh, Donzé, Fainekos, Maler, Nickovic, Sankaranarayanan, Specification-based monitoring of CPS (Lectures on Runtime Verification, LNCS 10457, 2018).** A survey of qualitative and quantitative (robustness) monitoring for STL and related logics, with a tools comparison. Its value here is the robustness semantics, which supplies a real-valued margin that both falsifiers and parameter-mining loops optimise, and a catalogue of monitoring algorithms whose bounded-horizon unrolling is what an SMT encoding of PolySTL reproduces.

**Bartocci, Mateis, Nesterini, Nickovic, Survey on mining STL specifications (Information and Computation 289, 2022).** Organises STL mining along four axes: whether the formula shape is fixed in advance or also learned, whether a model is available or only data, whether the miner can query the system (active) or only observe it (passive), and whether labels are available. In that taxonomy the delegation problem is model-based (the C++ code), active (BMC can be queried), supervised (counterexamples are labelled bad), and shape-learning (the grammar is open), which is the least populated cell; most surveyed methods are passive and data-driven and give no proof.

**Cimatti, Griggio, Mover, Tonetta, Parameter synthesis with IC3 (FMCAD 2013).** Extends IC3 to infinite-state symbolic transition systems with parameters and computes the region of parameter values under which an invariant holds, by repeatedly discovering and blocking parameter values that lead to a violation. Guarantees: if it terminates, the region is exact. Scalability: linear arithmetic, hybrid-system benchmarks. Relevance: it shows how to compute "for which constants does the code satisfy the property" inside a model checker, which is the analogue for (C) of parameter mining, if the BMC toolchain could expose parameters symbolically.

**Mohammadinejad, Deshmukh, Puranic, Mining environment assumptions for CPS models (ICCPS 2020).** This is the closest published match to computing A₂. The component's output requirement is given in STL; the method looks for an STL formula over the component's input signals that separates inputs whose outputs satisfy the requirement from those that do not. It enumerates PSTL shapes in increasing size and learns parameters with decision trees, labelling inputs by simulating the component. Guarantees: the result is a classifier with empirical accuracy, not a proof; there is no verification step. Scalability: enumeration over small shapes and simulation cost. The structure (enumerate shape, fit constants, label by running the component) carries over directly; the missing piece is replacing simulated labels with BMC verdicts so that the final assumption is checked.

**Gaaloul, Menghi, Nejati, Briand, Wolfe, EPIcuRus: mining assumptions for software components (ESEC/FSE 2020).** Learns an input assumption for a Simulink component under which a given requirement holds. Search-based test generation produces labelled input signals; a decision-tree learner turns them into a candidate assumption; a model checker for Simulink checks whether the requirement holds under that assumption, and if not, the loop continues with more tests. Guarantees: when the model checker accepts, the assumption is sufficient (sound relative to the model checker); no completeness. Scalability: across 18 industrial requirements it found an assumption for each, most within an hour. A later TSE paper by the same group replaces the decision tree with genetic programming. Relevance: this is the only published loop found that pairs learned assumption proposals for a control component with a formal check of sufficiency, i.e. condition (C), on an actual design artefact.

## 3. Assumption synthesis for reactive synthesis and for compositional verification

**Chatterjee, Henzinger, Jobstmann, Environment assumptions for synthesis (CONCUR 2008).** Given an unrealizable specification φ on a finite game graph, the goal is an assumption ψ about the environment only, as weak as possible, that makes ψ → φ realizable. The safety part is obtained by removing a minimal set of environment edges, computable in polynomial time; the liveness part adds fairness conditions on remaining environment edges, and finding a minimal such set is NP-hard, so the paper computes a locally minimal one using probabilistic games. Guarantees: sound and, for the safety part, minimal. Relevance: this is the game-theoretic statement of "the weakest assumption under which a component can meet its guarantee", which for M₂ (a fixed program rather than a strategy to be chosen) degenerates to computing the largest input set under which the code meets G, i.e. the largest A₂ satisfying (C).

**Li, Dworkin, Seshia, Mining assumptions for synthesis (MEMOCODE 2011).** For GR(1) specifications that are satisfiable but unrealizable, the environment's counterstrategy is a finite witness of unrealizability. The method mines candidate LTL assumptions from that counterstrategy, restricted to a fixed menu of syntactic patterns, adds one, and repeats realizability checking. Checker: a GR(1) synthesis tool. Guarantees: each added assumption excludes the current counterstrategy; the loop ends when the specification becomes realizable; completeness is relative to the pattern menu. Scalability is that of GR(1) synthesis (symbolic, polynomial in the state space). The counterstrategy-to-assumption step is the right analogue for condition (R): when the planner cannot reach A₂ from A, the counterstrategy says which planner outputs A₂ must additionally admit.

**Alur, Moarref, Topcu, Counter-strategy guided refinement of GR(1) specifications (FMCAD 2013).** Refines the same idea: candidate assumptions are generated from a fixed set of pattern shapes over the variables appearing in the counterstrategy, and the refinements are organised as a search tree, with checks that keep the added assumptions consistent so that the environment is not ruled out entirely. Guarantees: the procedure terminates because the pattern set over finite variables is finite; any returned assumption makes the specification realizable; completeness is relative to the patterns. Scalability: depends on counterstrategy size and branching of the refinement tree.

**Cobleigh, Giannakopoulou, Păsăreanu, Learning assumptions for compositional verification (TACAS 2003).** For the assume–guarantee rule "⟨A⟩ M₁ ⟨P⟩ and ⟨true⟩ M₂ ⟨A⟩ imply ⟨true⟩ M₁ ∥ M₂ ⟨P⟩", the assumption A is learned as a finite automaton by Angluin's L* algorithm. Membership and equivalence queries are answered by a model checker on M₁ and on M₂ in turn; counterexamples either refine A or expose a real violation. Guarantees: for finite-state components the loop terminates, and the learned automaton never exceeds the size of the minimal automaton for the weakest assumption; it either proves P or returns a genuine counterexample. Scalability: the learned automaton can be large, and each query is a model-checking call. This is the shape-free ancestor of interface learning in verification, since the automaton is learned without a fixed syntactic form; its two-sided structure (one component's check bounds the assumption from one side, the other component's check from the other) is the same sandwich as (C) and (R) in Section 0.

**Garg, Löding, Madhusudan, Neider, ICE learning (CAV 2014).** Reformulates invariant synthesis as learning from three kinds of samples: states that must be included (examples), states that must be excluded (counterexamples), and pairs (s, s′) meaning "if s is in, s′ must be in" (implications). The teacher is a verifier checking initiation, consecution and safety; each failed check yields one sample. Guarantees: sound by the teacher; for learners over finite or well-structured hypothesis classes that never repeat a refuted hypothesis, the loop converges. Relevance: the implication sample is precisely the shape of information a program verifier produces when a property is relative to an unknown, and the paper argues that without it the learner/teacher protocol is not robust.

**Ezudheen, Neider, D'Souza, Garg, Madhusudan, Horn-ICE (OOPSLA 2018).** Generalises implications to Horn samples (several antecedents, one consequent), which arise when inferring invariants and procedure contracts together in modular verification. The learner is a decision-tree algorithm over numeric predicates; the teacher is an SMT-backed program verifier. Guarantees: sound by the verifier, polynomial-time learning per round, convergence for the finite predicate pool. Relevance: delegation produces non-Horn samples on the planner side (for some environment input, at least one of several planner outputs must be in A₂), which is a disjunctive constraint that neither ICE nor Horn-ICE handles natively, but which an SMT-based learner over A₂'s parameters can encode directly.

## 4. Compositional synthesis with contracts

**Nuzzo et al., Contract-based methodology for aircraft electric power system design (IEEE Access 2, 2014).** Applies platform-based design with assume–guarantee contracts across abstraction levels: topology selection by mixed-integer optimisation, discrete supervisory control synthesised from LTL (reactive synthesis), and real-time requirements in STL checked by simulation and optimisation. Contracts are written by the designer; tools check compatibility and refinement and synthesise implementations per component. Guarantees: each step is sound relative to its tool; there is no automatic contract search. Relevance: it is the main worked example of top-down contract decomposition for a CPS where different components are handled by different engines, which is the situation here (BMC for M₂, a synthesiser for M₁).

**Incer Romeo, Sangiovanni-Vincentelli, Lin, Kang, Quotient for assume–guarantee contracts (MEMOCODE 2018);** and **Incer Romeo, Mangeruca, Villa, Sangiovanni-Vincentelli, The quotient in preorder theories (GandALF 2020, EPTCS 326).** The quotient C / C₂ is the most permissive contract for a missing component such that composing it with a component meeting C₂ refines the top contract C. The MEMOCODE paper gives a closed form for AG contracts over behaviour sets; the GandALF paper places it in a general algebraic setting. Relevance: Section 0's observation that (A₁, G₁) = (A, A₂) is the quotient specialised to the series rule used here. It removes step two from the search entirely: computing (A₁, G₁) costs nothing once (A₂, G₂) is known, and all the difficulty moves to (C) and (R).

**Incer et al., Pacti (arXiv 2303.17751, 2023; ACM TCPS 9(1), 2025, as "Pacti: Assume-guarantee contracts for efficient compositional analysis and design").** A tool that implements contract composition, quotient, merging and refinement for contracts whose assumptions and guarantees are conjunctions of linear inequalities, with algorithms that avoid full quantifier elimination. Guarantees: exact for the polyhedral fragment, with documented approximations where needed. Scalability: designed for many-component systems with polyhedral constraints. Relevance: it is a reference implementation of contract algebra; polynomial temporal constraints are outside its fragment, but its handling of the quotient is the model for the closed-form step.

**Kim, Arcak, Seshia, A small gain theorem for parametric assume–guarantee contracts (HSCC 2017).** Contracts whose guarantee is a function of a parameter that the assumption also constrains; finite-gain properties become such contracts, and a small-gain condition guarantees that a feedback interconnection of components meeting their parametric contracts meets the composite contract. Relevance: if the planner and controller interact in feedback (e.g. the planner re-plans from the vehicle state), circular assume–guarantee reasoning is unsound in general, and this paper gives one sound condition for closing such loops with parameterised contracts.

**Ghasemi, Sadraddini, Belta, Compositional synthesis via a convex parameterisation of AG contracts (HSCC 2020).** For networks of linear systems with polytopic constraints, the assumption sets (bounds on neighbours' states) and guarantee sets are parameterised as zonotopes, and the contracts and the local controllers are found together by one convex (linear) program. Guarantees: sound; conservative because of the fixed parameterisation; no CEGIS needed because the exists–forall is eliminated in closed form by the convex parameterisation. Scalability: linear programming, large networks. Relevance: the cleanest example where contract search and controller synthesis are solved jointly by a single optimisation, possible only because both components are models rather than code.

**Finkbeiner and Passing, Dependency-based compositional synthesis (ATVA 2020).** For LTL synthesis of multi-component systems, the system is ordered by a dependency analysis so that each component has a dominant strategy (one that does at least as well as any alternative) given the already synthesised strategies of the components before it. Guarantees: sound and complete for the class where such a decomposition exists. Relevance: a series system M₁ → M₂ is the degenerate case; the dominance idea maps to "the controller's behaviour is fixed (it is code), so the planner is synthesised against it".

**Finkbeiner and Passing, Compositional synthesis of modular systems (ATVA 2021; ISSE 18, 2022).** Synthesises, for each process, both an implementation and a certificate that describes what the process promises the others, so that each process is synthesised against the others' certificates rather than their implementations. Certificates are bounded in size, and the search over implementations and certificates is encoded as a SAT problem in the style of bounded synthesis. Guarantees: sound; complete up to the size bound; biasing towards small certificates favours small interfaces. Scalability: much faster than monolithic distributed synthesis when small certificates exist. Relevance: the certificate is the interface A₂; the size bound plays the role of the grammar depth bound.

**Majumdar, Mallik, Schmuck, Zufferey, Assume–guarantee distributed synthesis (IEEE TCAD 39(11), 2020).** For distributed reactive synthesis on finite game graphs, assumptions and guarantees for each process are computed by an iterative negotiation: when one process cannot win under the current assumption about the others, the minimal additional assumption it needs is computed and becomes a new obligation on the others, and the rounds repeat. Guarantees: sound but incomplete; terminates on finite graphs because assumptions only grow. Relevance: the negotiation protocol is the two-sided interface search of Section 0 run on games rather than on code.

**Anand, Nayak, Schmuck, Contract-based distributed logical controller synthesis (HSCC 2024; arXiv 2307.06212 as "Contract-based distributed synthesis in two-objective parity games").** Computes, for two players with separate parity objectives, pairs of permissive assumption and strategy objects whose compatibility yields fully decoupled local specifications: each player may then pick any local strategy meeting its local specification and the profile is winning. Guarantees: complete and terminating for finite parity games; the local specifications are maximally cooperative. Relevance: shows that, in finite-state settings, the interface search can be made complete; nothing comparable exists when one component is real-valued code checked by BMC.

## 5. Synthesis from STL and AG-style specifications for CPS

**Raman, Donzé, Maasoumy, Murray, Sangiovanni-Vincentelli, Seshia, MPC with STL specifications (CDC 2014; arXiv 1703.09563).** Encodes bounded-horizon STL as mixed-integer linear constraints over a discrete-time linear model, with robustness as a linear objective, and solves it in receding horizon. The search is over control input sequences; the checker is the MILP encoding itself, so any solution satisfies the specification on the model by construction. Guarantees: sound and complete relative to the discrete-time model and horizon (MILP is exact); recursive feasibility needs separate arguments. Scalability: number of binaries grows with formula size times horizon; tens of steps are routine. Relevance: this is the natural way to synthesise M₁ for the quotient obligation (A, A₂) when A₂ is in an STL fragment, though PolySTL's polynomial predicates make the encoding mixed-integer nonlinear unless predicates are linearised.

**Raman, Donzé, Sadigh, Murray, Seshia, Reactive synthesis from STL (HSCC 2015).** Adds an adversarial environment: a CEGIS loop alternates between a controller MILP that finds inputs robust against a finite set of environment behaviours and an adversary MILP that searches for an environment behaviour violating the specification under the current inputs; found behaviours are added to the set. Guarantees: if the loop ends with no counterexample, the inputs are correct against all environment behaviours over the horizon; termination is not guaranteed in general for continuous disturbance sets. Relevance: this is the forall–exists half of the delegation problem (condition R) solved by exactly the CEGIS pattern of Section 1, with MILP in both roles.

**Sadraddini and Belta, Robust temporal logic MPC (Allerton 2015).** For discrete-time linear systems with bounded additive disturbances, constructs MILP-based controllers that satisfy STL constraints for every disturbance realisation, with a fallback to a minimally violating relaxation when the constraint is infeasible. Guarantees: sound and conservative (disturbance handled by set-based tightening rather than enumeration). Scalability: better than CEGIS on disturbances because the forall is eliminated by tightening. Relevance: an alternative to the HSCC 2015 loop for condition (R) when A is a bounded disturbance set.

**Ghosh, Sadigh, Nuzzo, Raman, Donzé, Sangiovanni-Vincentelli, Sastry, Seshia, Diagnosis and repair for synthesis from STL (HSCC 2016; arXiv 1602.01883).** When the STL-to-MILP problem is infeasible, extracts an irreducible infeasible subset of constraints, maps it back to the responsible subformulas, and suggests minimal parameter relaxations that restore feasibility; in the reactive case it combines this with the CEGIS loop and with assumption mining for the environment. Guarantees: the repaired specification is realizable on the model; minimality is with respect to the slack objective. Relevance: when (R) fails for a proposed A₂, this is the mechanism that tells the proposer which parameter of A₂ to loosen and by how much, i.e. the feedback channel from the planner side.

## 6. Learned proposers inside verification loops

**Wu, Barrett, Narodytska, Lemur (ICLR 2024).** Formalises the cooperation between an LLM and an automated program verifier as a set of transition rules over proof states: the LLM proposes candidate invariants or intermediate assertions, the verifier checks them, and proved facts become assumptions for the next step. The authors prove the rules sound, so whatever the LLM proposes, a final "verified" verdict is justified by the verifier alone. Completeness is not claimed. The instantiation used existing C verifiers and improved results on synthetic and competition (SV-COMP style) benchmarks. Relevance: Lemur is the right correctness argument for an LLM proposer of A₂: if every accepted fact passes through the checker, the LLM's unreliability affects only efficiency.

**Kamath et al., Finding inductive loop invariants using LLMs (arXiv 2311.07948, 2023)** and **Chakraborty et al., Ranking LLM-generated loop invariants (Findings of EMNLP 2023).** The first builds a benchmark of verification problems and shows that LLM-proposed invariants, filtered by a sound symbolic checker and combined with a symbolic baseline, solve more problems than the baseline. The second trains a ranker so that the checker is called on the most promising LLM samples first. Guarantees: soundness from the checker; ranking only reduces checker calls. Relevance: in delegation the expensive oracle is BMC on ArduPilot, so ordering proposals before checking matters more than generating many.

**Wu, Cao, Yao, Wei, Chen, Ma, LLM meets bounded model checking: neuro-symbolic loop invariant inference (ASE 2024).** Asks the LLM for many candidate invariants, splits them into individual predicates, uses BMC to discard predicates that are falsified on bounded runs, recombines the surviving predicates into new candidates, and then checks inductiveness with an SMT solver, feeding failures back into the next prompt. Guarantees: sound by the final SMT check; BMC is used only as a cheap filter. Scalability: solved 309 of 316 benchmark programs against 218 for the best baseline. Relevance: the "propose many, filter with BMC, recombine, then prove" pipeline is directly reusable for proposing conjuncts of A₂.

**Pirzada, Reger, Bhayat, Cordeiro, LLM-generated invariants for BMC without loop unrolling (ASE 2024).** Uses LLM-proposed loop invariants so that ESBMC can verify C programs without fully unwinding loops, replacing each loop by an invariant-based abstraction whose validity is itself checked. Guarantees: sound when the invariants are checked; turns bounded checking into unbounded proof for the covered loops. Relevance: ArduPilot's navigation code is called from a periodic scheduler; a horizon-N PolySTL check needs N iterations of the main loop, and this technique is a route to avoiding N-fold unrolling.

**Tihanyi, Charalambous, Jain, Ferrag, Cordeiro, Towards self-healing software via LLMs and formal verification (AST 2025; arXiv 2305.14752).** ESBMC finds a violation and produces a counterexample trace; the code and trace are given to an LLM that proposes a fix; ESBMC re-checks. Guarantees: a fix is accepted only if the BMC check passes within its bounds. Relevance: shows the plumbing for feeding ESBMC counterexample traces to an LLM, which is the feedback path needed when (C) fails for a proposed A₂.

**Pirzada, Wang, Charalambous, Korovin, Cordeiro, ConVer (arXiv 2605.27051, 2026).** Top-down compositional verification of C programs: from a system-level assertion, an LLM proposes function contracts; system-level checks (assuming the contracts) and function-level checks (proving each contract on its function) alternate in a combined abstraction-refinement and inductive-synthesis loop, with contracts refined by an ICE-style learner when checks fail; the backend is ESBMC. Guarantees: sound when both levels pass; incomplete. Results: high success on simple Frama-C benchmarks, partial success on parsers and recursive programs. Relevance: this is structurally the closest existing system to the delegation loop, except that its contracts are pre/post-conditions of C functions rather than temporal formulas over signals.

**Chen, Lin, Godbole, Singh, Polgreen, Lee, Seshia, PolyVer (FMCAD 2025; arXiv 2503.03207).** Models a multi-language system as a transition system whose update functions are C or Rust code; pre/post-condition contracts for the update functions are proposed by SyGuS solvers or by LLMs, checked against the code by language-specific verifiers (CBMC for C, Kani for Rust), and then used as abstractions by a system-level model checker (UCLID5). The loop interleaves abstraction refinement (when the system-level check fails spuriously) with inductive synthesis (when a contract is too weak or not provable). Guarantees: sound when it terminates with a proof; it also reports genuine system-level counterexamples. Relevance: PolyVer is the best precedent for the exact split in the delegation problem: a code-level obligation checked by a C bounded model checker, a system-level obligation checked by a logic tool, and synthesised contracts as the glue, with both a grammar-driven and an LLM proposer.

**Li, Parsert, Polgreen, Guiding enumerative program synthesis with LLMs (CAV 2024).** Integrates LLM calls into a weighted probabilistic enumerator for SyGuS: LLM outputs update the grammar's weights, and the enumerator's progress is fed back to the LLM. Guarantees: the final answer is SMT-checked; completeness of the enumerator is retained. Results: the combination outperformed the LLM alone, the enumerator alone, and the SyGuS competition winner. Relevance: this is how to keep grammar completeness while using an LLM: the LLM biases a complete enumerator rather than replacing it.

**Sun, Sheng, Padon, Barrett, Clover (SAIV 2024, LNCS 14846).** Checks consistency among code, natural-language docstring and formal annotations in Dafny, using the verifier for code-to-annotation consistency and LLM-based reconstruction tests for the other directions. Guarantees: code–annotation agreement is verified; the natural-language links are heuristic. Relevance: an independent check that a proposed specification is not merely provable but also matches intent, which matters because a too-weak A₂ that M₂ trivially satisfies is useless for the planner.

**Cosler, Hahn, Mendoza, Schmitt, Trippel, nl2spec (CAV 2023, LNCS 13965).** Translates natural-language requirements into LTL with an LLM, exposing sub-translations so a human can correct ambiguous fragments interactively. There is no solver check of correctness; validation is human. Relevance: a front end for producing the top-level (A, G) or the vocabulary of A₂ from ArduPilot documentation, not a part of the verified loop.

**Wen et al., AutoSpec (CAV 2024, LNCS 14682).** LLM-proposed specifications (pre/post-conditions, loop invariants) for C programs, guided by static analysis that decomposes the program into small pieces, with each proposal validated by a deductive verifier (Frama-C/WP) before being kept, so errors do not accumulate. Guarantees: sound for accepted specifications. Results: about 79% of benchmark programs verified, and an application to a real X.509 parser. Relevance: the decomposition step (ask the LLM about one call-graph node at a time) is what makes proposal on a code base the size of ArduPilot plausible.

**Misu, Lopes, Ma, Noble, AI-assisted synthesis of verified Dafny methods (FSE 2024)** and **Yang et al., AutoVerus (OOPSLA 2025).** The first measures how well LLMs produce verified Dafny methods with specifications and proof hints from a problem statement, with the Dafny verifier as the filter. The second uses several LLM agents for initial proof generation, refinement and verifier-error-guided repair for Rust/Verus, reaching over 90% of a 150-task benchmark. Guarantees: soundness from the verifier. Relevance: evidence that verifier error messages are effective feedback for an LLM, which supports feeding BMC traces (not just pass/fail) back to the A₂ proposer.

**Cao et al., LLM2Cert (arXiv 2610.30524, 2026).** An LLM proposes Lyapunov and barrier certificates for nonlinear and high-dimensional systems; each proof obligation is sent to an SMT solver or, when SMT stalls, to a Lean proof-repair agent; feedback from failed obligations drives new proposals. Results: 81 of 105 benchmark instances verified, a 17-point gain over one-shot proposals. Relevance: the continuous-dynamics analogue of Lemur, and a data point that feedback rounds, not single proposals, carry the gains for real-valued certificate search.

**ArjomandBigdeli, Zhou, Bak, LLMs as falsifiers for CPS (arXiv 2609.20752, 2026).** Uses an LLM as the optimiser in STL falsification, giving it signal names, trajectories and the time of minimum robustness; on ARCH-COMP benchmarks it needed fewer simulations than other falsifiers on 14 of 21 specifications. Relevance: a cheap counterexample source for (C) before calling BMC: falsify a candidate A₂ by simulation first and reserve BMC for candidates that survive.

## 8. The loop I would build for M₁ → M₂

Following Section 0, the only search variable is the interface A₂, a PolySTL formula over the waypoint-command signals that M₁ emits and M₂ consumes (waypoint positions, leg lengths, turn angles at waypoints, commanded speeds, timing between updates). G₂ is then G (or G ∪ ¬A₂), and (A₁, G₁) is the quotient (A, A₂). The loop has three proposer/checker pairs.

Step 1, shape proposal. Proposer: an LLM that reads the navigation code and its parameters (speed, acceleration and waypoint-radius settings, the code paths that clamp or reject commands) and proposes candidate A₂ skeletons from a fixed PolySTL grammar, each with symbolic constants: for example, "every leg is at least d long", "every turn angle is at most θ", "commanded speed stays below v", "consecutive waypoints arrive at least τ apart", "every waypoint lies inside the fence polygon". To retain grammar completeness, the LLM should bias a complete enumerator over the grammar (Li–Parsert–Polgreen) rather than be the only source. Checker at this stage: Z3 checks that each skeleton is well-formed, mentions only M₂'s inputs (receptiveness), and is satisfiable together with what A says about the planner's environment.

Step 2, constants against the code (condition C). For a skeleton with constants p, the obligation is ∀ behaviours: inputs ⊨ A₂(p) and M₂'s run ⇒ G. Discharge the universal quantifier by BMC on a harness: the harness drives the ArduPilot navigation entry points for the PolySTL horizon, constrains inputs with assume statements generated from A₂(p), and asserts G. The existential over p is discharged outside BMC by CEGIS: each BMC counterexample is a concrete input trace on which M₂ violates G; that trace becomes a constraint "trace ∉ A₂(p)", which is a quantifier-free polynomial constraint on p alone, because the trace is concrete. The parameter learner is Z3 (or dReal if transcendental functions appear) solving for p that excludes all collected bad traces while keeping A₂ as large as possible; with PSTL monotonicity (Asarin et al., Jin et al.) "as large as possible" becomes bisection along each monotone parameter. This is CEGIS(T) with the program kept entirely on the checker side: the learner never sees code, only traces. Before each BMC call, cheap falsification by simulation (SITL, or an LLM falsifier) removes obviously bad p. The pure-logic entailment G₂ ∩ A₂ ⊆ G is trivial once G₂ := G, and A ⊆ A₁ is trivial once A₁ := A.

Step 3, the planner side (condition R). With A₂(p) fixed, M₁'s obligation is the quotient (A, A₂(p)). If M₁ is synthesised, use bounded-horizon STL-to-MILP/MINLP synthesis (Raman et al. 2014) with an adversary loop over environment behaviours in A (Raman et al. 2015), or a robust tightening (Sadraddini–Belta) when A is a bounded disturbance set; either way the forall over environment behaviours is discharged by the adversary solver, and the existential over planner outputs by the controller solver. If M₁ is hand- or LLM-written code, check M₁ ∩ A ⊆ A₂(p) by BMC on M₁'s code, the same way as step 2. When (R) fails, the infeasibility core (Ghosh et al. 2016) or the adversary's counterexample gives an environment behaviour e ∈ A for which no admissible planner output lies in A₂(p); this yields a disjunctive constraint on p ("at least one of these output traces must be admitted") that is again quantifier-free in p and goes back to the learner of step 2.

So the learner over p receives negative samples from the code (traces A₂ must exclude) and disjunctive positive samples from the planner (sets of traces of which A₂ must admit one). It is an ICE-style learner whose samples are not limited to Horn form, which Z3 handles directly as a finite disjunction. If the learner reports that no p satisfies both sides, the skeleton is refuted and step 1 proposes another; the unsat core of the learner's constraints says which bad and needed traces clash, which is useful feedback to the LLM. Termination: guaranteed if the grammar depth is bounded and parameters are searched to a tolerance ε; within those bounds the loop is complete, outside them it is not. Soundness of the final delegation rests on three checks only: BMC for (C) (and for M₁ if it is code), the synthesiser or BMC for (R), and Z3 for the quotient algebra, which is trivial. Nothing the LLM produces is trusted.

Where the exists–forall alternation is discharged: the outer ∃A₂ is split into ∃skeleton (LLM-biased enumeration) and ∃p (Z3 over finitely many concrete traces); the inner ∀ behaviours of M₂ is discharged by ESBMC/CBMC, once per candidate, as an unsatisfiability check; the inner ∀ environment behaviours of the planner is discharged by the adversary solver (or by BMC on M₁); and the CEGIS loop is what converts the alternation into a sequence of quantifier-free queries.

## 9. Weakest link

The weakest link is condition (C), specifically what "a behaviour of M₂" means inside the BMC harness. Three things make the code-level verdict weaker than the logic-level ones. First, bounds: BMC proves the property only up to the unwinding and horizon chosen, and the ArduPilot main loop, the navigation library's internal loops and its numerical routines (square roots, trigonometry, floating point) must either be fully unwound for the PolySTL horizon or replaced by checked loop invariants (Pirzada et al. 2024); unwinding assertions must be enabled or the proof is silently partial. Second, the harness and stubs: the navigation code depends on attitude/position estimation, the scheduler, parameter storage and motor output, all of which must be stubbed, and every stub encodes an unrecorded assumption that does not appear in A₂. The contract is only as strong as the harness, and nothing in the loop checks the harness. Third, the signal gap: PolySTL speaks about sampled signals, while BMC speaks about program states; the mapping (which variables are the signals, at which call sites, with which sampling period) is written by hand. If G mentions the physical vehicle (positions reached, separation from obstacles), M₂ alone does not determine it; a plant model must be added either into the harness or into A, and that model is outside the code that was verified. By comparison, the LLM is not a soundness risk at all in this design; its weakness is incompleteness, meaning that a failure to find A₂ does not show that none exists in the grammar unless the enumerator, rather than the LLM, exhausted the bounded grammar. In short: the logic and the search are the strong parts; the code-to-signal harness for ArduPilot is where the guarantee can quietly fail, and it deserves its own review, its own tests against SITL runs, and an explicit list of stub assumptions carried into the final contract.
